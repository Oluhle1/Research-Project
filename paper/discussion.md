# VI. Discussion

## A. How common is reported non-receipt, and how fragile is that answer?

The screened cohort does not show widespread reported non-receipt. About seven in every hundred apparently eligible adults report that they do not receive the Older Person’s Grant, and the weighted figure is close to the unweighted one. Under the observable rules used here, reported receipt is the usual situation in KwaZulu-Natal in 2014/2015.

That statement is about a constructed denominator. One hundred and eighty-seven adults aged 60 or older remain unresolved, and 117 fail a known rule. The unresolved group is larger than the non-recipient group. Most of the unresolved adults are married people whose spouse is not in the household, or people recorded as living together. The primary estimate is therefore the rate among people whose income, partnership, and grant answers can be resolved. It is not the rate among every older adult, and it is not the rate among everyone who might qualify under the full legal test [1].

The estimate moves little when the income ceiling, the price basis of income, or the receipt period is changed. It moves more when adults who report an own disability grant are kept in the cohort. That exclusion is a legal feature of the grant, because a person does not receive the Older Person’s Grant and another social grant for themselves at the same time [1]. It is also the assumption that does the most work in the number. Partnership rules change who is counted by several dozen people, but those added adults mostly report receipt, so the proportion barely changes. The practical lesson is that the headline rate is fairly stable to the rand threshold and is more sensitive to who is treated as already receiving an incompatible grant.

This is a narrower question than much of the South African grant literature. Godfrey and colleagues ask which eligible households receive grants and describe demographic correlates of receipt [6]. Ralston and colleagues compare rural pensioners with rural non-pensioners [2]. Studies of the Child Support Grant describe application barriers, distance, and exclusion of young mothers [9], [10], [14], [15]. Those papers establish that receipt is uneven and that dealing with the grant system is costly [12], [13], [16]. They do not supply an OPG non-receipt rate for a screened KwaZulu-Natal population in the Living Conditions Survey. The present estimate fills that specific gap. It should not be read as evidence that administrative burden is small. The survey does not record applications, documents, waiting time, or payments. People who report receipt may still have faced those costs [16], [17].

The uncertainty around 7.5% is wide. The sampling-unit approximation runs from about 5% to 10%, and it does not use strata. A difference of a few percentage points would sit inside that interval. The unresolved pool is a second, separate uncertainty: it is a group the screen cannot classify, not a sampling error.

## B. What the characteristics predict, and what they do not

The six characteristics support a moderate ranking of reported non-receipt and only a small improvement in probability error. An area under the ROC curve of 0.75 means the model orders non-recipients ahead of recipients more often than a coin toss. Average precision of 0.20 has to be read next to the 6.6% prevalence [20]. A researcher who looked only at the ROC figure would overstate how often a high predicted probability is a true non-recipient. In the highest fifth of predicted risk, about one in six adults reports non-receipt. Most adults in that fifth report receipt.

The Brier score falls from 0.062 to 0.058 relative to predicting the same risk for everyone. That is a real gain and a small one. Average calibration is close to the diagonal [19]. The calibration slope is 0.84, and the interval around it includes both a clearly over-sharp model and a slope of 1. With 67 events, the study can support a cautious claim of some ranking information. It cannot support a precise claim about how well the probabilities are scaled. Riley and colleagues’ sample-size guidance is consistent with that limit [21].

The predictive information is concentrated in sex and schooling. In the full-sample fit, women and adults with schooling below Grade 7 have substantially lower odds of reported non-receipt. Age, settlement, and distance to a welfare office have wide intervals that include no association. Settlement and welfare access were included because the access literature treats place, travel, and the difficulty of reaching an office as part of grant receipt [9], [15], [16], [17]. Inside this already screened cohort, those variables do not show a clear association. Schooling was included for a similar reason: less schooling might mark an information barrier. The association runs in the other direction. Among adults who already pass the observable means test, less schooling goes with higher reported receipt, not with non-receipt. One reading that stays short of a cause is that, once people above the income ceiling are removed, lower schooling marks adults with little other income who do receive the grant. The data do not separate that reading from other explanations, and they do not show that low schooling blocks access in this cohort.

Replacing welfare-office access with a married indicator does not improve the held-out probabilities. A survey-weighted fit does not improve them either, and its calibration slope is weaker. Changing the eligibility cohort moves the ROC area only between 0.74 and 0.76. The characteristics therefore contain some stable ranking information, mostly demographic, and they do not contain a sharp individual prediction.

The model cannot say why a person did not receive the grant, whether the characteristics came before non-receipt, or whether the same ranking would appear in another province, year, or administrative file [2], [6]. Internal validation respects the household clustering in this survey [22]. It is not an external test. Reporting both the ROC area and the calibration results follows the distinction those metrics require [19], [23].

## C. Does the artefact make the analysis inspectable?

The command-line artefact does the job set for the minimum version. One command rebuilds the cohort, the weighted description, and the held-out predictions. Two runs wrote the same files. Twenty-six synthetic decisions, stated before the run, matched the program, including the income boundary, a missing spouse, an incompatible grant, and a caregiver grant that must not set the label. A researcher can read, for a named survey person, the status, the reason codes, the rule version, the fold, and the held-out probability.

That is transparency of the research pipeline. It is not yet a test of whether another person finds the files easy to use. The review screen and the task of asking someone to locate a reason or read a calibration display were left until the core command worked. The artefact therefore supports RQ3 as a reproducible and inspectable analysis. It does not yet support a claim about usability by a reader who does not open the tables.

The same limit applies to the substantive claim. The program explains how this study screened people. It does not decide who should receive the grant [1].

## D. What remains weak

Three results should stay visible. First, the unresolved group is larger than the non-recipient group, so the choice of denominator is as important as the rate itself. Second, the model’s improvement over a single risk is small, and a high score is still usually a recipient. Third, the access variables suggested by the grant literature are the terms this sample does not pin down, while sex and schooling are precise and, for schooling, point away from a simple information-barrier story. Those limits are informative. They are the result the study was prepared to report if the available survey characteristics could not predict non-receipt sharply [21], [23].

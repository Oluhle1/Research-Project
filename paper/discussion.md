# VI. Discussion

## A. How common is reported non-receipt, and how fragile is that answer?

The screened cohort does not show widespread reported non-receipt. About seven in every hundred apparently eligible adults report that they do not receive the Older Person’s Grant, and the weighted figure is close to the unweighted one. Under the observable rules used here, reported receipt is the usual situation in KwaZulu-Natal in 2014/2015.

That statement is about a constructed denominator. One hundred and eighty-seven adults aged 60 or older remain unresolved, and 117 fail a known rule. The unresolved group is larger than the non-recipient group. Most of the unresolved adults are married people whose spouse is not in the household, or people recorded as living together. The primary estimate is therefore the rate among people whose income, partnership, and grant answers can be resolved. It is not the rate among every older adult, and it is not the rate among everyone who might qualify under the full legal test [1].

The estimate moves little when the income ceiling, the price basis of income, or the receipt period is changed. It moves more when adults who report an own disability grant are kept in the cohort. That exclusion reflects the restriction on simultaneous own OPG and incompatible grants [1]; the screen does not exclude an adult for a caregiver child grant or grant-in-aid. It is also the assumption that does the most work in the number: S3 adds 19 people, 15 of them non-recipients, whereas the broader single-person partnership screen adds 68 people and only two non-recipients. Reporting denominator, events, both descriptive rates and validation metrics together prevents stability of AUC from hiding a substantive change in who is counted. The practical lesson is that the headline rate is fairly stable to the rand threshold and is more sensitive to the treatment of an incompatible own grant.

This is a narrower question than much of the South African grant literature. Godfrey and colleagues ask which eligible households receive grants and describe demographic correlates of receipt [6]. Ralston and colleagues compare rural pensioners with rural non-pensioners [2]. Studies of the Child Support Grant describe application barriers, distance, and exclusion of young mothers [9], [10], [14], [15]. Those papers establish that receipt is uneven and that dealing with the grant system is costly [12], [13], [16]. They do not supply an OPG non-receipt rate for a screened KwaZulu-Natal population in the Living Conditions Survey. The present estimate fills that specific gap. It should not be read as evidence that administrative burden is small. The survey does not record applications, documents, waiting time, or payments. People who report receipt may still have faced those costs [16], [17].

The uncertainty around 7.5% is wide. The sampling-unit approximation runs from about 5% to 10%, and it does not use strata. A difference of a few percentage points would sit inside that interval. The unresolved pool is a second, separate uncertainty: it is a group the screen cannot classify, not a sampling error.

## B. What the characteristics predict, and what they do not

The five predictor domains, represented by six slopes, support a moderate ranking of reported non-receipt and only a small improvement in probability error. An area under the ROC curve of 0.75 means the model orders non-recipients ahead of recipients more often than a coin toss. Average precision of 0.20 has to be read next to the 6.6% prevalence [20]. A researcher who looked only at the ROC figure would overstate how often a high predicted probability is a true non-recipient. In the highest fifth of predicted risk, about one in six adults reports non-receipt. Most adults in that fifth report receipt.

The Brier score falls from 0.062 to 0.058 relative to predicting the same risk for everyone. That is a real gain and a small one. Average calibration is close to the diagonal [19]. The calibration slope is 0.84, and the interval around it includes both a clearly over-sharp model and a slope of 1. With 67 events, the study can support a cautious claim of some ranking information. It cannot support a precise claim about how well the probabilities are scaled. Riley and colleagues’ sample-size guidance is consistent with that limit [21].

The predictive information is concentrated in sex and schooling. In the full-sample fit, women and adults with schooling below Grade 7 have substantially lower odds of reported non-receipt. Age, settlement, and distance to a welfare office have wide intervals that include no association. In the conceptual framework, schooling is a limited proxy for possible information/learning costs, while settlement and welfare-office distance are limited proxies for travel and compliance costs [9], [15], [16], [17]. Sex and age describe demographic position; they are not direct measures of administrative burden. These predictors are conceptually distinct from income and partnership rules, which define the observable denominator, and from reported receipt, which defines the model outcome. The artefact exposes those distinctions through status, reason codes and a separately labelled probability.

Inside this already screened cohort, the place/access variables do not show a clear association. Less schooling goes with higher reported receipt, rather than the simple information-barrier pattern. One interpretation that stays short of a cause is that, once people above the income ceiling are removed, lower schooling marks adults with little other income who do receive the grant. Conditioning on the observable screen can also change the associations seen among all older adults. The data do not distinguish these explanations. Applications, document demands, waiting times, psychological costs and administrative decisions are not observed; no measured association identifies a burden mechanism or tests the whole theory.

The primary population-description target and the prediction target require separate interpretation. Person weights raise the descriptive non-receipt rate from 6.61% to 7.49%; primary validation evaluates unweighted held-out people. Replacing welfare access with a married indicator produces nearly the same AUC and Brier score. The frequency-weighted sensitivity fit has a Brier score of 0.0583 and slope of 0.718, compared with 0.0578 and 0.836 for the primary model. Applying person weights to the same primary predictions gives different metrics without adding new validation data. None of these comparisons establishes superiority of one fitted model. Changing the screening and validation assumptions leaves AUC between 0.736 and 0.763, but the event rate and Brier score vary. The available characteristics contain some stable ranking information, mostly demographic, and they do not contain a sharp individual prediction.

The model cannot say why a person did not receive the grant or whether the characteristics came before non-receipt [2], [6]. Internal validation respects household clustering in this survey [22]. Reporting both ROC area and calibration follows the distinction those metrics require [19], [23]. Internal partitioning and sensitivity analyses remain tests within the historical source population, rather than independent external tests.

## C. Does the artefact make the analysis inspectable?

The artefact now comprises a reproducible command-line pipeline and a local Streamlit review interface. One command rebuilds the cohort, weighted description and held-out predictions; two runs wrote the same 14 files. The original 26-check log includes 22 synthetic screening cases and four checks of income schedules, forbidden predictors and run tracing. The later 220-test suite and native-UI comparison of all 15,388 reference records provide additional correctness evidence. They are related verification layers, not 246 independent screening decisions.

The interface makes the decisions inspectable through upload validation, classifications, reason codes, prediction displays and audit-bearing exports. A supported CSV is processed using the same historical rules; filters change the displayed/exported subset while the summary denominator remains the full uploaded resolved-pass group. Generated row labels replace submitted identifiers, and raw income is absent from result displays and exports. The project owner's Windows walkthrough and the 50 checks of six supplied report files demonstrate that the local synthetic workflow completed and produced consistent exports. They do not establish broad usability or user acceptance. No consenting representative-user sample, timed task outcomes or ease ratings has been collected. The planned small-sample synthetic-data protocol remains a research activity to complete before making participant-usability claims.

The application stores frozen full-cohort coefficients and preprocessing statistics, while the research evaluation retains household-held-out probabilities. Agreement after serialization is an inference check; uploaded scores are full-data fitted scores and do not create a new held-out or external evaluation. Reported receipt is not a predictor, a probability cannot change a screening result, and no upload triggers model fitting. The program explains how this study screened records. It does not decide who should receive the grant [1]. RQ3 is supported for correctness, reproducibility and inspectability, with participant usability still unestablished.

## D. External validity and remaining limitations

The data represent a screened KwaZulu-Natal LCS 2014/2015 population. Other provinces can differ in settlement, access and grant administration; later periods can differ in thresholds, policy, coverage and reporting. Administrative records measure application/award/payment events differently from survey-reported receipt and can have different missingness and linkage errors. The historical upload schema deliberately constrains dates and study context; successful software validation does not establish suitability for contemporary applicants. The retained model has not been externally validated in another province, time period or administrative setting. Transport would require a compatible target definition, justified local screening rules and independent performance/calibration assessment; software deployment alone does not supply that evidence.

Three results should stay visible. First, the unresolved group is larger than the non-recipient group, so the choice of denominator is as important as the rate itself. Second, the model’s improvement over a single risk is small, and a high score is still usually a recipient. Third, the access variables suggested by the grant literature are the terms this sample does not pin down, while sex and schooling are precise and, for schooling, point away from a simple information-barrier story. Those limits are informative. They are the result the study was prepared to report if the available survey characteristics could not predict non-receipt sharply [21], [23].

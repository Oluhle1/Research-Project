### Introduction: problem definition and aim

Age eligibility, resource eligibility and reported receipt are different states. An older adult reporting no OPG may have resources above the programme threshold, may not have applied, may be awaiting a decision, or may have incomplete survey information [1]. An age-only denominator cannot distinguish these possibilities. Income, marital circumstances and missing partner resources can change who enters a survey-based apparently eligible population. The research problem is therefore both substantive and measurement-related: estimating reported non-receipt requires an explicit, inspectable denominator before either a percentage or a prediction can be interpreted.

The aim was to develop and evaluate an interpretable computational artefact for analysing reported OPG non-receipt among apparently eligible older adults in KwaZulu-Natal using LCS 2014/2015. Its objectives were to construct a documented person-level population, describe receipt using person weights, internally validate a parsimonious model, and assess the artefact’s correctness and reproducibility. RQ1 addresses prevalence and screening assumptions; RQ2 addresses prediction; RQ3 addresses reproducibility and inspectability. Representative-user clarity remains unevaluated.

### Related work: review provenance and comparative evidence

The structured review documented in Submission 1 used thematic narrative synthesis. Searches covered Scopus, Dimensions, IEEE Xplore, SABINET/African Journals Online, Google Scholar, and relevant Statistics South Africa and DataFirst resources, with concepts spanning social assistance, eligibility, take-up, non-receipt, exclusion and prediction. The original review reported 511 records, 136 duplicates, 375 screened records, 153 full-text assessments and 55 retained sources after 98 full-text exclusions. These are the previously reported broader social-grant review counts, not a new search or 55 OPG-specific studies. Studies were compared by programme, setting, analytical unit, outcome and limitations. Their heterogeneous definitions support narrative comparison rather than a pooled non-receipt estimate.

Ralston et al. [2] compare pension receipt among age-eligible adults in rural Agincourt. Their person-level outcome motivates studying receipt differences, but age eligibility does not establish the complete means test, and one rural locality cannot directly describe KwaZulu-Natal. Godfrey et al. [6] examine rural and peri-urban Msunduzi households; their outcomes are proportions of age-eligible household members receiving grants. Geographic relevance therefore coexists with a different analytical unit: a household association need not equal an individual OPG probability. Raniga and Simpson [7] address pension-supported livelihoods in Bhambayi, while Alloush et al. [8] examine protection during a later crisis. Such consequence studies explain why access matters without supplying the present person-level screened denominator.

The stages of access also differ. Case et al. [9] consider grant contact, including enquiry and application, while Zembe-Mkabile et al. [10] distinguish application from receipt among mothers of young infants in Umlazi. Heinrich and Brill [13] study interruptions among people already receiving the Child Support Grant. These outcomes are not interchangeable with OPG non-receipt among screened older adults. They nevertheless show why non-receipt at one observation need not imply rejection, permanent exclusion or absence of prior contact. Current receipt and preceding-year income must therefore remain separately interpreted measures.

Gazeaud [11] examines proxy-means-test vulnerability to measurement error in Tanzania. That system differs from the South African research income screen, so its findings do not establish local legal rules or quantify error in LCS. The transferable methodological point is that resource measurement can alter classification, motivating explicit income-definition sensitivities. Mabala’s logistic analysis of Social Relief of Distress Grant outcomes among young applicants [18] offers a modelling comparison, but a different grant, age range and applicant-based sample cannot validate OPG probabilities. Both comparisons reinforce the need to define population and event before interpreting model performance.

### Research gap and methodological connection

The reviewed evidence leaves a focused integration task, rather than an absence of pension research: combine observable eligibility, grant-specific reported receipt, survey-weighted description and internally validated prediction within one reproducible person-level KwaZulu-Natal analysis. Direct OPG evidence informs receipt patterns; other-grant evidence informs possible access processes and methodological caution. Income and consumption can partly reflect grants, so direct receipt fields and outcome-derived variables must not become predictors. The artefact makes the screen, receipt label and separate probability inspectable, allowing their assumptions and limited empirical support to remain visible [19]–[23].
